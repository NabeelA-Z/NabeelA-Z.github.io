USB-C Li-Ion Charger & Boost Converter PCB

KiCadUSB-C Li-ion chargingBoost converter LTspice2-layer PCB

Work in progress. Schematic capture, connector selection, and layout are done. Fabrication, assembly, and bring-up are still ahead. This page tracks the design as it goes.

1.0Why This Board Exists

This is the first hardware module of a larger build: a mini scanning rover, a small mobile robot with LiDAR / time-of-flight scanning capability. Rather than start with the MCU and drivetrain and bolt power on at the end, I designed the power path first as a standalone, reusable module. Charge a Li-ion cell from USB-C, then step that cell voltage up to a stable rail that can run the rover's electronics.

Designing it as a separate board has a second payoff. It is a complete from-scratch PCB exercise in its own right, covering schematic capture, connector selection against real datasheets, power electronics, and layout, without the rest of the rover clouding the scope.

2.0Signal Chain

The board is four stages in series, and every design decision below traces back to one of them:

  1. USB-C input. 5 V in from a standard charger, with the port correctly identifying itself as a power sink.
  2. Li-ion charge IC. Takes VBUS and manages the charge profile into the cell.
  3. Boost converter. Takes the cell voltage, which sags across the discharge curve, and holds a stable higher output rail.
  4. Output connector. Carries the regulated rail off-board to the rover electronics.

3.0USB-C Input Stage

The input connector is a GCT USB4105 series USB-C receptacle, using the KiCad footprint USB_C_Receptacle_GCT_USB4105-xx-A_16P_TopMnt_Horizontal. Selecting it meant cross-referencing the manufacturer datasheet against the library footprint rather than trusting the name alone, since a 16-pin top-mount horizontal part has to match both the pin map and the mechanical footprint at the board edge.

The port is wired power only. VBUS and GND go to the charge circuit. The detail that matters is the configuration channel: CC1 and CC2 are each pulled down to GND through 5.1 kΩ. Those two resistors are what tell an attached source that this is a standard USB-C power sink asking for default current. Leave them off and a compliant charger will never turn VBUS on, so a two-resistor omission is enough to make the whole board dead on arrival.

D+, D−, and the remaining unused pins are left unconnected, with explicit no-connect flags placed in the schematic so ERC treats them as a deliberate decision instead of an oversight.

Design note

The two 5.1 kΩ resistors are the cheapest components on the board and the most consequential. USB-C sink detection is not optional wiring, it is the handshake that makes the port deliver power at all. They are also placed physically tight to the connector in layout, for the same reason the schematic bothers with them.

4.0Charge and Boost Path

The charge IC stage sits directly off VBUS and manages charging the Li-ion cell, which connects through its own battery connector so the pack can be swapped or removed from the board. The boost converter then takes that cell voltage and steps it up to the output rail.

The boost stage is the part that has to be engineered rather than simply wired. A single Li-ion cell falls from roughly 4.2 V down toward 3.0 V over a discharge, and the rover's electronics need a fixed rail regardless of where the cell sits on that curve. That means the converter's inductor, switch node, and feedback network have to be chosen for the full input range, not just a nominal operating point, which is exactly what the simulation work in section 7.0 is for.

5.0Output Connector

The regulated output leaves the board on a second connector, planned as Mini-USB to match the Mini-USB power input on the target MCU board. Choosing the connector to fit the downstream hardware, rather than dropping in a generic header, keeps the rover build cable-simple later on.

Like the input, it is wired power only: VBUS and GND carry the boost output, and D+, D−, and ID are left no-connected. The footprint under consideration is USB_Mini-B_Lumberg_2486_01, or an equivalent Mini-USB-B receptacle from the same KiCad library, pending a final mechanical check.

6.0PCB Layout

Placement was done by function, not by convenience, working along the signal chain:

  • USB-C input at the board edge, so the cable plugs in from outside the board rather than over it.
  • CC pull-down resistors right at the connector, keeping the configuration lines short.
  • Charge IC clustered near the VBUS entry, so the input power path stays short before it does any work.
  • Boost converter section kept tight, with the inductor, switch node, and feedback grouped together and separated from the noisier areas of the board.
  • Output connector placed last, at the end of the chain.

Routing followed the same priority split. Power nets (VBUS, battery, boost output) are routed wide and direct to hold down resistance and drop at current. The sensitive nets, meaning the CC lines and the boost feedback, are deliberately routed away from the switching node, since feedback is the one trace on this board where picked-up switching noise turns straight into a regulation problem.

The board is two layer, with vias used to hop from front copper (F.Cu) to back copper (B.Cu) wherever a route could not stay on one side. Working through this surfaced a constraint worth writing down: an SMD pad exists on only one copper layer, so a via cannot simply be dropped onto the pad the way it can on a through-hole pin. The via has to sit just off the pad, with a short trace connecting the two.

The board outline is defined on Edge.Cuts to physically bound the PCB, sized with clearance around the placed components. Mounting holes are still an open question. They cost nothing but board area, they cannot be added after the board is fabricated, and on a vehicle that moves there is a case for bolting the module to the chassis rather than letting it hang off its own cable. The decision gets made before the fabrication files go out.

7.0Simulation Approach

Boost converter behaviour is being validated in LTspice before component values are frozen: switching waveforms, inductor current, output ripple, and stability. I started in KiCad's built-in ngspice simulator and moved to LTspice after hitting simulator setup problems that were costing more time than the simulation itself.

One deliberate simplification in the model: the USB-C receptacle is replaced with a plain 5 V DC source. A connector is a mechanical part with no meaningful SPICE behaviour to solve for, so modelling it adds nothing. What matters electrically at that point in the circuit is that 5 V is present on VBUS, and that is exactly what the source provides.

8.0Background Applied

This board builds on earlier embedded and circuits work rather than starting cold. The 3D spatial mapper put me on the VL53L1X time-of-flight sensor over I²C and UART on an MSP432E401Y, which is directly the sensing front end the rover will reuse, and TM4C1294 firmware work covers the MCU side. Transistor-level MOSFET and BJT analysis is what makes the boost converter's switch node something to reason about instead of a block to copy. The DC power supply project is the same discipline at a smaller scale: design the power path on paper, simulate it, then measure it.
