Henkelpott: Champions League Prediction Engine

PythonNumPy / SciPy TypeScriptReact Web WorkersMonte Carlo Statistical modelling

1.0The Question

Henkelpott, "the pot with handles", is the German nickname for the Champions League trophy. The project asks a narrow question: given everything known about these 36 clubs today, how likely is each one to lift it in Madrid on 5 June 2027?

Answering that properly is two problems stacked on top of each other. The first is rating: how many goals should Bayern expect against Bodø/Glimt, when the two clubs almost never play each other and spend the season in leagues of very different strength? The second is structure: the 2024/25 format change replaced the group stage with a single 36-club Swiss-model league, a tie-breaker cascade, a knockout play-off and a fixed bracket. A title probability has to come out of simulating that whole path, not from rating the clubs alone.

The constraint that shaped everything

A probability is only worth publishing if it can be checked. That meant two commitments from the start: test the model on seasons it never saw against a credible baseline, and make every simulation reproducible, so a shared link shows exactly the numbers the sender saw.

2.0System Overview

The work splits cleanly across a language boundary. Fitting is heavy, slow and done once per matchday, so it lives in Python. Simulating is fast, interactive and done every time someone opens the page, so it lives in the browser.

  1. Ingest. fetch.py downloads domestic results from football-data.co.uk and European results from openfootball; parse.py merges them into one match table with one ID per club across every source.
  2. Fit. fit.py fits the goals model and an Elo baseline, runs the backtest, and freezes the ratings, fixtures and validation results into a single model.json.
  3. Simulate. A static React app loads that file and runs the season thousands of times in Web Workers. Nothing is fetched at runtime.

3.0The Goals Model

Every match is modelled as two Poisson goal counts. Each club has an attack and a defence rating, and the expected goals for each side come from a log-linear model:

log λhome = μ + atthome − defaway + H
log λaway = μ + attaway − defhome

Independent Poisson goals are known to get low scores wrong: real football has more 0–0 and 1–1 draws than the product of two Poissons predicts. The Dixon-Coles correction rescales the four cells 0–0, 1–0, 0–1 and 1–1 by a factor controlled by one parameter, ρ, fit after the ratings. It came out at ρ = −0.053, which nudges probability toward low-scoring draws in the expected direction.

Recent results count more

Squads change every summer, so a result from 2016 should not count as much as one from last month. Every match is weighted by e−ξ·age. The tuned value, ξ = 0.003 per day, gives a half-life of about eight months: last season matters, three seasons ago barely does.

Home advantage, measured twice

Home advantage is a fitted parameter, not an assumption, and it is fit separately for domestic and European football. It came out noticeably larger in Europe: H = 0.21 in league play, H = 0.33 in European competition, so a home side in Europe is expected to score about 39% more than on neutral ground. That matters for the simulation, because every two-legged tie is decided partly by who hosts the second leg. The final in Madrid is neutral and gets H = 0.

Fitting it fast enough to tune

With 752 clubs and 56 countries the model has a little over 1,600 parameters. The negative log-likelihood and its full gradient are written out by hand in vectorised NumPy, with np.bincount scattering per-match residuals back onto clubs and countries, and handed to SciPy's L-BFGS-B. Analytic gradients are what make the backtest in section 5.0 practical: the hyperparameter search refits the model 32 times, and the test then refits it once per season.

4.0Putting Every League on One Scale

This is the hardest problem in the project. A domestic result only says how strong a club is relative to its own league. Winning the Norwegian league by fifteen points and winning the Premier League by fifteen points are not the same achievement, and a model trained naïvely on both would treat them as if they were.

The fix is to split each rating into two parts: attclub = Acountry + αclub, and likewise for defence. The club deviation α is shrunk toward zero with an L2 prior (σ = 0.9); the country term A is barely shrunk at all. Domestic matches only ever compare clubs with the same country term, so they cannot move A. The only data that can pin the country terms down is matches where leagues meet: European competition.

That is why the training set includes the Europa League, the Conference League and all the qualifying rounds, not just the Champions League. Those 3,762 European matches are where most of the information about Austria, Norway, Slovakia or Azerbaijan lives, because those leagues' clubs rarely reach the Champions League proper but meet everyone in the earlier rounds.

Design decision

The shrinkage is asymmetric on purpose. Strong shrinkage on clubs and weak shrinkage on countries tells the optimiser that individual clubs should stay close to their league's level unless the data clearly says otherwise, while leaving the league levels free for the European results to set. Shrinking both equally would have let domestic dominance leak into the cross-league comparison.

Joining the data sources

None of this works unless "Man City" in one file and "Manchester City FC" in another are the same row. parse.py gives each club a single COUNTRY:Name ID using the European-competition spelling, matches domestic names to it with normalised fuzzy matching, and falls back to a hand-written table of about fifty fixes where fuzzy matching is unreliable ("Sp Lisbon", "Nott'm Forest", "Buyuksehyr"). One club split across two IDs would quietly get two half-trained ratings and break the cross-league link it was supposed to carry.

5.0Validation

The backtest is built to avoid fooling myself. For each season from 2021/22 to 2025/26, both the model and the Elo baseline were frozen on 1 September using only data from before that date, then asked for win/draw/loss probabilities on every Champions League match of that season. The hyperparameters (ξ, σ and a European-match weight) were chosen on 2019/20 and 2020/21 only, then left alone. No test season influenced any choice.

The headline metric is the ranked probability score, which, unlike plain accuracy, rewards a model for being close: predicting a draw when the home side wins is penalised less than predicting an away win.

Overall the model's RPS is 1.7% better than Elo's. That is a modest margin, and the season breakdown is more informative than the total. Elo actually won two of the five seasons. The model's advantage is concentrated in 2024/25 and 2025/26, the two 36-club seasons, where the Swiss format pairs clubs from very different leagues far more often than the old group stage did. That is exactly the situation the cross-league calibration in section 4.0 was built for, so the result lines up with the reason the model is structured the way it is.

The calibration figure says something different from the RPS. An expected calibration error of 2.3% means that when the model says an outcome is 40% likely, it happens close to 40% of the time. For a tool whose whole output is a list of probabilities, being calibrated matters as much as being sharp.

Reading the result honestly

Elo is a strong baseline and a 1.7% improvement is not a breakthrough. I report the per-season table, including the seasons the model lost, because the total on its own would hide where the improvement comes from. Only two seasons have used the new format so far, so the evidence that the model handles the format better rests on 378 matches.

6.0Simulating the Season

Ratings give match probabilities. Title odds come from playing the whole competition many times. Each simulated season runs:

  1. League phase. All 144 fixtures. In season to date mode, results that have already happened are locked in and only the remaining fixtures are simulated.
  2. The table, using UEFA's full tie-breaker order: points, goal difference, goals scored, away goals scored, wins, away wins, then opponents' points, goal difference and goals scored.
  3. Knockout play-off for 9th to 24th, paired 9/10 v 23/24 down to 15/16 v 17/18, with the pairing within each group drawn.
  4. Fixed bracket from the round of 16, with each seeded pair split across the two halves so 1st and 2nd can only meet in the final.
  5. Two-legged ties with no away-goals rule. Level after 180 minutes goes to 30 minutes of extra time at the second-leg venue, each side's scoring rate scaled to a third of a match, then a kick-by-kick penalty shootout that stops as soon as the result is decided.
  6. The final, a single match on neutral ground.

Writing the bracket as data rather than logic paid off. The play-off pairings and the round-of-16 paths are two small lookup tables in bracket.ts, and the same function builds the bracket for the simulator, for the user's own predictions and for the PNG poster export, so the three can never disagree about who plays whom.

7.0Making It Fast and Reproducible

Two simulators, one set of rules

The engine has two season simulators. The detailed one builds full match objects with lineups, substitutions, goal timelines and player ratings, for the one "featured" season shown in the Match Center. The fast one, fast.ts, runs the same model and the same rules over preallocated typed arrays, allocates almost nothing per season, and is what the bulk Monte Carlo uses. Three details carry most of the speed:

  • Precomputed samplers. Each fixture's scoreline grid is computed once, sorted by probability and stored as a cumulative array, so drawing a score is one random number and a short linear scan that usually stops within a few cells.
  • A packed sort key. The first six tie-breakers are packed into a single number per club, so ranking the table is one numeric comparison in the common case instead of a cascade of six.
  • Cached extra-time rates per pair of clubs, since the same knockout pairings recur constantly across runs.

Parallel without losing determinism

Runs are split into fixed chunks of 1,000 and spread across a pool of Web Workers (hardware threads minus one, capped at six), with results passed back as transferable typed arrays so nothing is copied. The obvious way to do this breaks reproducibility: if each worker owns one random stream, the answer depends on how many workers the visitor's machine has.

Instead every chunk gets its own random stream seeded from seed/agg/<chunk>, using a cyrb128 hash feeding an sfc32 generator. Chunks are merged in chunk order regardless of which worker finished first. The result is identical on a 4-core laptop and a 16-core desktop, and there is a unit test that asserts exactly that.

Why it matters

The seed, run count and mode all live in the URL as ?seed=…&runs=…&asof=…. A link reproduces the run exactly, which turns any quoted title probability from a claim into something anyone can check by opening the link.

8.0The App

The front end is a static React and TypeScript site built with Vite and deployable to any static host, and is live on Vercel. It has five main views:

  • Dashboard: title odds, the 36-to-1 funnel, advancement odds per club, and the model's backtest credentials up front.
  • Predict: a drag-and-drop 1–36 table and full knockout bracket, plus a one-match predictor with a two-leg mode.
  • Match Center: every match of the featured season, the table, a position-by-matchday chart, lineups and pre-match scoreline odds.
  • Live Bracket: an animated bracket reveal with a PNG poster export.
  • Methodology: the model, the validation tables and a calibration chart, all read from the same model.json the simulator uses, so the documentation cannot drift out of date from the numbers.

Grading a prediction fairly

The bracket builder grades the user's prediction, and the obvious way to do it, scoring it against one simulated outcome, is mostly noise. Instead the entry is scored against every simulated season already run for the dashboard, and compared with a "chalk" entry that picks the model's favourite everywhere. The grade is the ratio of the two expected scores. The report also gives the chance of beating chalk, the best and worst cases, the safest and boldest picks, and the odds of the whole bracket coming true. A brave bracket is not penalised for being brave, only for being expensive in expectation.

9.0Testing

The engine has a Vitest suite covering the parts where a silent bug would produce plausible-looking but wrong probabilities:

  • Score grids sum to 1 and two-leg tie odds sum to 1.
  • The fixture list has 144 matches with exactly 8 per club.
  • The tie-breaker cascade ranks correctly, including away goals breaking a tie on goal difference and goals scored.
  • Shootouts always produce a winner with a legal score.
  • The bracket follows the seeded paths and keeps 1st and 2nd apart.
  • Simulations are deterministic for a seed and stable under different chunking.
  • Season-to-date mode locks in real results.
  • A bracket of pure favourites grades close to chalk.

10.0Known Limitations

Where the build stands
ItemState
SquadsPlaceholders ("Liverpool No. 9"); the format for real UEFA A-lists is documented and ready
Elo baselineBuilt in-house to Club Elo's design, because Club Elo's API was down at build time
Coefficient tie-breakerThe final, rarely reached tie-breaker is approximated by pot and draw order
Data gapsNo domestic data after 2024/25 for Azerbaijan, Slovakia and Ukraine; those clubs lean on European results
Model refreshManual: enter scores, rerun fetch.py and fit.py (about 30 s), redeploy
Player modelGoals are shared among players by role weights, not by individual player quality

What I would change

Load real squads so the player statistics mean something. Automate the matchday refresh with a scheduled job that refits and redeploys. Then move past team-level ratings: the model knows nothing about injuries, rotation or transfers, and every one of those shows up first as a change in who is on the pitch.

11.0What This Project Taught Me

The modelling was the most interesting part, but the most important decision was the validation setup: freezing the model at a fixed date, tuning on seasons the test never touched, and reporting the seasons the model lost. Without that, a 1.7% improvement is unverifiable. With it, the improvement is small, real, and explained by the part of the model designed to produce it.

It also showed me how much engineering sits between a correct model and a usable one. The maths fits on one line. Making it run tens of thousands of seasons in a browser tab, give the same answer on every machine, and explain itself to someone who has never heard of a ranked probability score was most of the work.

The approach carries over from my hardware projects. In the BJT amplifier the design is only trusted once simulation and measurement agree; here the model is only trusted once it beats a baseline on data it has never seen.
