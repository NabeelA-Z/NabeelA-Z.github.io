Assistive Vision Discord Bot

PythonYOLOv11 PaddleOCRdiscord.py OpenCVAccessibility ENGINEER 1P13

1.0The Client & The Brief

ENGINEER 1P13: Power in Community pairs teams with a real client instead of a hypothetical one. Ours was a 61-year-old woman with Usher Syndrome, a condition that impairs both vision and hearing. Critically, neither sense is entirely gone, and the condition is progressive, so what she can see and hear today is not what she will be working with in a few years.

Her stated goal was independence: living without constant supervision from others. The daily task she raised was grocery shopping, where the specific failure is not navigation but identification. She could get to the store and could not reliably tell what she was picking up or putting in the cart. Existing aids she had access to did not address that.

Why this brief is harder than it looks

A dual sensory impairment removes the usual fallback. An assistive tool for a blind user can lean entirely on audio. One for a deaf user can lean entirely on visuals. Here neither channel is reliable on its own, so every response has to go out both ways at once and be usable if only one of them lands. That single constraint drove most of the design decisions on this page.

2.0The Solution

A software-only vision aid with two functions, split along how grocery items actually present themselves:

  • Object detection for unlabelled goods. Loose produce, fruit and vegetables, anything with no printing on it to read.
  • Text reading for labelled goods. Soup cans, boxed cereal, packaged items where the answer is written on the front and just needs to be read out.

Those are two different computer vision problems, so they get two different models rather than one compromise. The whole thing runs on the phone the client already owns, with no additional hardware to carry, charge, or lose.

3.0Why Discord

Building a custom mobile app is the obvious answer and for this project, was the wrong one. It would have meant writing an accessible camera flow, an accessible results screen, and a speech layer from nothing, then asking the client to learn proprietary software no one had ever used.

Discord was chosen on three arguments:

  1. The accessibility work is already done and already tested. Discord ships text-to-speech, zoom up to 300%, contrast and saturation controls, and colour settings, all maintained by a company with real accessibility resources.
  2. It survives a progressive condition. This is the strongest argument and the one specific to this client. Her vision and hearing will continue to change. Because Discord's accessibility settings are adjustable, the same deployment can be re-tuned as her needs shift, rather than being frozen at whatever contrast and text size were correct on the day it was handed over. A custom app would have needed us to come back and rebuild it.
  3. Help is available from people who are not us. Discord has an enormous user base. If the client gets stuck, support exists in the world already. With a custom app, it is a lot harder to find help.

Discord is also light on storage, which matters on a phone that has to hold it permanently.

Design decision

Choosing an existing platform over a custom build is a scope decision, not a shortcut. It traded control over the interface for a solution the client could use on day one, on hardware she already owned, that stays viable as her condition changes. The team's effort went into the vision pipeline and the response design instead of into rebuilding an interface that already existed and was already accessible.

4.0System Architecture

The code splits into an engine and the interface that drives it, which kept the vision work independent of the platform it was delivered on:

  • main.py holds a ComputerVision class. This is the whole engine: fetch an image from a URL, run detection, run OCR. It has no idea Discord exists.
  • bot.py holds ListenerBot, a subclass of discord.Client. It handles the platform: listening for messages, routing them by channel, and shaping the reply.

Images arrive as URLs, not files. When the client attaches a photo in Discord, the bot receives a link, so get_image_from_url pulls the bytes with requests, wraps them in a numpy byte array, and hands that to cv2.imdecode to produce an OpenCV image. Nothing ever touches disk.

5.0The Vision Engine

Object detection uses YOLOv11 nano through the Ultralytics API. get_objects predicts on the decoded image and returns two things from one call: the list of detected class names, which becomes the spoken sentence, and the annotated image with boxes drawn on it, which becomes the visual reply. Returning both together means the model runs once per photo rather than twice. The prediction is capped at a 0.60 confidence floor, which is the single most consequential number in the project. Lowering it would catch more objects and start narrating things that are not there. For someone deciding what to put in a cart based on what the bot says, a confident wrong answer is a worse failure than no answer, so the threshold is deliberately set to stay quiet when unsure.

Text reading uses PaddleOCR with English and angle classification enabled, the latter because a photo taken by someone who cannot see the viewfinder is very often rotated. get_text flattens the nested OCR result down to just the recognised strings, joins them into one line, and falls back to an explicit "No recognised text." rather than an empty string. That sentinel is what the bot checks before deciding whether to speak at all.

Both models were picked for being lightweight and fast rather than for being the most accurate available. Someone standing in a grocery aisle holding up a can is waiting on the answer, so latency is a usability property here, not a benchmark number. YOLO also sits on top of OpenCV, and all three libraries are well documented, which for a first-year team is a real selection criterion rather than a nice-to-have.

6.0Interaction Design

This is the part of the project that is not about machine learning, and it is where most of the real design work sits.

Channels as modes

There are no commands to memorise. The bot decides what to run based on which channel the photo was posted in: one for object recognition, one for text recognition. The client picks a service by picking a room. For a user who cannot comfortably scan a menu, that is a lower cognitive load than a command syntax, and it is impossible to mistype. As section 8.0 covers, this layout was not the original design. It came out of testing.

Every answer, twice

Because neither sense can be relied on alone, each response goes out on both channels at once. The visual reply is prefixed with #, Discord markdown for a level-one heading, so the text renders at the largest size the app will produce. The same content is posted, unformatted, into a dedicated text-to-speech channel, and read aloud into a voice channel the client joins before using the service. She reads it, hears it, or both.

Making the speech sound like a sentence

A starts_vowel helper checks the first letter of a detected label and picks "a" or "an" accordingly, so the bot says "there is an apple in front of you" instead of "there is a apple". It is four lines of code, and when the output is audio it is the difference between something that sounds like a device and something that sounds like a person.

Filtering the answer, not just the detections

YOLO detects people, and in a grocery store it detects the client and every shopper near her in almost every frame. If more than one object is found and "person" is among them, the bot removes it and re-checks whether a single object is left. Being told "there is a person" on every photo is noise that buries the part of the answer she actually asked for. The phrasing then adapts to what survives: one object gets a full sentence, several get a comma-separated list, and none gets an explicit "no objects detected" rather than silence.

Always saying what state it is in

The bot adds a 👍 reaction to the incoming photo, then announces "waiting for image" once it has replied. Detection is not instant, and without a signal the client cannot distinguish "thinking" from "broken". The idle announcement means the system is never silently doing nothing.

7.0Testing & Results

Both functions were measured quantitatively, and the interface was tested on people through surveys.

Quantitative results
MetricResult
Average image processing time129 ms
Object detection, correct trials70%
Object detection, average confidence (all trials)54.5%
Text recognition accuracy89%

That confidence figure needs its methodology stated, because it sits below the 0.60 floor the bot enforces and would otherwise look impossible. Confidence was recorded for every trial, including the ones where the model failed to clear the threshold and therefore said nothing to the user. So 54.5% is the model's raw certainty across everything it was shown, not the certainty of the answers that actually reached the client, which by definition all sat above 0.60.

Measuring it that way is the more honest choice. Reporting only the detections that passed the filter would have flattered the system by hiding every photo it quietly declined to answer, and for an assistive tool those silences are part of the user experience, not an absence of data.

The 129 ms processing time confirms the model choice: the response is effectively immediate from the user's side, and the wait she actually experiences is network and Discord, not inference.

The more interesting result is the split between the two functions. Text reading landed at 89% while object detection managed 70%, and that gap maps cleanly onto the client's own use case. Reading a soup can is a constrained problem with a right answer printed on it. Identifying loose produce is open-ended, and a nano model trained on general object classes is being asked to distinguish things it was never specifically trained to separate. The function serving labelled goods is the one that works well.

Qualitative survey results (out of 5)
CriterionRatingFeedback
Ease of use3.0Most participants struggled to input an image the first time, and expected it to get easier once learned

A 3.0 on ease of use is the honest number and the useful one. The audio output was landing, and the barrier was the step before it: getting a photo into the right place. That result is what drove the changes in the next section.

8.0What Changed After Testing

Two iterations came directly out of user feedback, and both are cases where the original design was defensible on paper and wrong in someone's hands.

One channel became two

The first build had a single channel, recognition-and-reader, handling both functions. It was fewer moving parts and it confused people, because the same room did two different things depending on what you sent it. Testers could not form a clear model of what the bot would do with their photo.

Splitting it into object-recognition and text-recognition made the choice explicit and physical. You pick the service by walking into the room for it. The channel-as-mode design described in section 6.0 is the outcome of this change, not the starting point.

The formatting character broke the speech

The # prefix that makes text render huge is the correct call visually. It turned out to be actively harmful audibly: it confused Discord's native text-to-speech output. The one character doing the visual accessibility work was damaging the audio accessibility.

Rather than dropping the large text, the fix was to separate the two paths entirely. Spoken output moved to a dedicated voice channel, fed by a plain unformatted copy of every message posted to a separate TTS text channel. The visual reply keeps its # and stays oversized; the audio path never sees the formatting character at all. The dual output survived, and each channel got to be optimised for its own medium.

The lesson

Both iterations came from watching people use the thing, not from reading the code. A markdown character that is correct in one output medium and broken in another is not something a test suite catches, and neither is a channel layout that makes perfect sense to the people who built it.

9.0Tradeoffs & Known Limitations

Where the build stands
ItemState
Bot token handlingLoaded from a .env file via python-dotenv, kept out of source
Channel IDsHard-coded, so redeploying to another server needs manual edits
HostingRuns on a laptop, so the service is only up while that machine is on
Model weightsYOLOv11 nano committed to the repo, so the bot runs without a download step
OCR callRuns twice per message in the text path, once to test the result and once to send it
Object detection accuracy70%, the weaker of the two functions and the obvious target for improvement
Alternate front endA Streamlit app was explored early, never used, and left in the repo

The hosting limitation is the one that matters most for a client who wants independence. A bot tied to a laptop being switched on is not the same as a service that is simply there, and moving it onto something always-on is the difference between a working prototype and something she could rely on.

What I would change

Host the bot somewhere always-on so it does not depend on a laptop staying awake. Move the channel IDs and the token together into configuration so it can be redeployed without touching source. Cache the OCR result instead of running PaddleOCR twice on the same image. And given that the 70% figure is the weakest number in the project, fine-tune detection on actual grocery items rather than relying on general-purpose classes.

10.0What This Project Taught Me

The technically interesting part is a detection model and an OCR engine wired to a chat platform. The genuinely hard part was everything downstream of the prediction: what to say, what to leave out, how to phrase it so it sounds like language, and how to deliver it to someone who cannot fully rely on either seeing or hearing it. None of that is visible in an accuracy number.

It also taught me that designing for a progressive condition means designing for a moving target. The strongest decision in the project was choosing a platform whose accessibility settings can be re-tuned as the client's vision and hearing change, so the solution does not expire the moment her needs shift.

The pattern carried forward: pick the platform that removes the work you do not need to be doing, then spend what you saved on the part only you can build. The same instinct shows up in the 3D spatial mapper, where the VL53L1X handles ranging so the effort goes into the scan and reconstruction pipeline instead.
