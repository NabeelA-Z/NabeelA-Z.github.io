MOSFET Analog Switch Design & Characterization

PMOS / NMOSLTSpice AD3 / WaveFormsBreadboarding ELECENG 2EI4

1.0Overview

An ideal switch has zero on-resistance, infinite off-resistance, no voltage limits, and works in both directions. Real switches have none of those properties — the engineering is in knowing how far a real design falls from ideal, and which non-idealities matter for the application. This project designed two MOSFET-based switches for 0–5 V operation and quantified each against the ideal: RON, off-state leakage, usable voltage range, and bidirectionality.

2.0Testing Methodology

Each parameter was tied to a specific measurement:

  • RON — measure VON across the switch and ION through it during the ON state; RON = VON/ION.
  • IOFF (leakage) — measure the voltage across the load resistor in the OFF state and apply Ohm's law; drain–source voltage gives ROFF.
  • Voltage range — sweep VIN from 0 to 5 V in 0.5 V steps and record VOUT to find where the switch passes the signal faithfully.
  • Bidirectionality — swap source and load between the two switch terminals, re-measure VON and ION, and record the percent difference.

All measurements used a 1 kHz square-wave control signal from the AD3's waveform generator, with the scope, cursors, and math channels in WaveForms for extraction.

3.0Switch 1 — Single PMOS

The first design is deliberately minimal: one PMOS pass device with its bulk tied to a separate 5 V supply. Simplicity buys two things — very low on-resistance and full bidirectionality, since with the bulk tied high, either non-gate terminal can act as source or drain.

The 2.5 V lower limit is the fundamental PMOS constraint: the device only conducts when |VGS| exceeds |VT|, so low input voltages can't turn it on. Within its range, though, it operates in the linear (triode) region with very small drop between terminals.

4.0Switch 2 — Two PMOS + NMOS

The second design attacks Switch 1's limited range. A three-transistor topology (two PMOS, one NMOS) extends the usable range at the output node to the full 0–5 V — verified with a VIN sweep showing faithful tracking from 0 V (VOUT = 0) up to 5 V (VOUT = 5.00 V).

The cost is bidirectionality: because one PMOS gate is driven by the output of another, the topology only works in one orientation. A SPICE simulation with the terminals swapped confirmed the failure — the output that should have oscillated between 2.5 and 5 V did not track correctly.

Engineering judgment

Neither switch is "better" — they occupy different points on the design space. Switch 1 wins where simplicity and bidirectionality matter and signals stay above 2.5 V; Switch 2 wins where full-range operation is required and current only flows one way (which covers most practical switching applications). Both RON values are negligible next to the 470 kΩ resistors in the surrounding circuit, so neither meaningfully loads the signal path.
